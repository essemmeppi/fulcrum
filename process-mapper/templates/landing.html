Making government processes legible to machines, operable by agents, and accountable to people.

October 2026 · working note

Government runs on processes. Who checks what, in which order, under which rule, by which deadline: this is what a public administration is, more than its buildings or its staff. Most of it is public by law, in statutes, guidance, forms and reports. It is written for people to read, one document at a time.

AI agents can now carry out long sequences of work in software, within rules, using tools. For a government, that capability is usable only where the process has been made explicit enough for a machine to follow and for a person to check. Almost nowhere has it been.

We make government processes legible. We read the public record of a service and compress it into a map: steps, actors, rules, data, flows, each element tied to the passage it comes from, with a measure of how sure we are. The compression is lossy. The loss is shown, not hidden. A process that is legible in this way can be operated by agents under human control, verified against the law it implements, and redesigned by comparison with others.

We began with one benefit in Finland.

1 · Map

Read everything the state publishes about itself.

Laws, guidance, forms, decision templates, internal instructions, annual reports. For one service that is a handful of documents; for a government, hundreds of thousands. We read them with frontier models in two passes. First, atomic facts, each with a verbatim quote that code checks against the source: a fact that cannot be found word for word is dropped. Then the map, assembled only from verified facts. Steps the documents do not show are marked inferred, not hidden.

The output is one JSON document per service: steps, actors, rules with their parameters, data sources, flows, each with its provenance and a confidence score. The schema is open and uses the words governments already use for services and processes, so a map reads the same in any country.

2 · Encode

Turn the map into something agents can run.

Each step says what an agent may do: execute it (deterministic rules, register lookups, notifications), prepare it for a human (document checks, discretionary assessments), or stay out (formal decisions, the citizen's own actions). Rules become testable statements with parameters and legal basis; data sources carry how they are reached.

This is the specification an agent harness loads: a tool per register, the rules as checks, an approval gate wherever the law puts a human. It is built on what agents can now do, long-horizon tool use, structured outputs, evaluation suites, so every run can be replayed and audited against the map. A process encoded this way is a verifiable task: an agent's run can be checked step by step against the law it implements.

3 · Redesign

Change the process once you can see it whole.

Redesign in government has meant workshops: the process is reconstructed from interviews, drawn once, and argued over. With many services encoded in one schema, the shared work shows itself: intake, identity, income verification, decision notice, annual review appear in every benefit, with rules that differ only in parameters and handovers that exist only because two offices once did.

Redesign becomes a comparison between maps rather than a workshop. Each proposed change is itself a map, with the same provenance and confidence, so it can be tested on synthetic cases before anyone changes a law or moves a desk. The drawing on the right is the mechanical version of this: steps that share an actor and a kind of work merge into one engine. We have not tested redesign with an agency yet.

What a step is

A map is a list of steps and the flows between them. Every step carries the same features, so maps can be compared, checked and loaded by software without interpretation.

Type
An event (something happens: an application arrives, a deadline passes), work by one actor, or a decision where the path splits.
Actor
Who performs it: citizen, caseworker, the authority's system, a third party, another authority.
External · internal
Whether the citizen sees the step, or it happens inside the authority.
Rules
The conditions and calculations the step applies, each a testable statement with parameters and legal basis. Rules are defined once per service and referenced by steps.
Inputs · outputs
What the step consumes and produces.
Data sources
The registers it reads, and how they are reached: by interface, by a person, or supplied by the citizen.
Channel · deadline
How the citizen interacts; the time limit that binds the step.
Agent mode
Execute, prepare for a human, or human only, with the reason.
Provenance
The passages the step rests on: source, section, verbatim quote. A step with none is marked inferred.
Confidence
Our expected probability that the step matches how the process really runs, from a fixed rubric. A responsible official can validate a step; validated steps count as certain.

    The test is a process owner at Kela reading the map. If they correct more than a third of the internal steps, the public record is not enough for the inside of the process and the method needs the agency's own documents from the start. If they correct few, the map can be validated in days rather than built in months. Until that reading happens, every number above is an estimate of how the process works, not a measurement.
